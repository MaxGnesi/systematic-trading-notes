Sample and shared definitions as in Lecture 22: Bybit BTCUSDT perpetual, 20 September - 3 November 2025; in sample 20 September - 9 October, out of sample 11 October - 3 November, 10 October apart; bootstrap over days (2,000 resamples), one-sided p-values, Benjamini-Hochberg at 5%.

## The styles

- **H10 (a sniper's footprint, and why it matters for our layers).** Orders sent within 100 ms after a move of Binance's price of 2 bp or more, in the same direction, have a larger net R(10 s) than other orders of the same size class, out of sample. Such orders are informed about a price that has already moved elsewhere, and ours would not be. If H10 holds, the impact layer is calibrated without them, and the difference is reported. Races themselves cannot be seen: the losers leave no trace.
- **H11 (trend followers at the close of a bar, and makers who expect them).** A whole minute is a **mark**; the **trend** at a mark is the sign of the last minute's closing mid against the average of the last 50 closes. Two tests, out of sample:
  - *H11a, flow with the trend.* Taker buys less sells, multiplied by the trend's sign, in the first 3 s after a mark is larger than three times its average per second in seconds 10 to 49 of the same minute.
  - *H11b, makers widen before the mark.* The share of quotes with a spread wider than one tick is larger in the last 2 s before a mark than in seconds 10 to 49 of the same minute.
  - *The test of each:* per out-of-sample day, the mean excess over the day's whole minutes less its mean over **fake marks** 10 to 50 s after the minute (every 500 ms), each fake still compared with seconds 10 to 49 of its own whole minute; then a one-sided t over the days.

  Reported, no test: the extra taker size in the first 3 s, whatever its side (the burst of Kim and Hansen, 2026, arXiv 2607.09426, preprint, found on Binance with Bybit as a check); the spread in the first 3 s after; all of it by mark (minute, 5 minutes, quarter-hour, hour), with the funding times (00:00, 08:00, 16:00 UTC) left out and reported apart; the trend also as the 20 against the 50 closes. The marks :00 and :30 of Lecture 19's depth jumps are the same clock. What a success shows: flow at the close of a bar that leans with the trend, consistent with bar-based trend rules, not proof of them. A failure says little: in the simulation the flow of such traders is partly taken back by others.
  *Changed again on 2026-11-07, before any out-of-sample data was used.* H11b first used the average spread and both tests a t over days. In the simulation (step 5), with no clock at all, fake marks off the minute passed |t| > 2 for the average spread in 13 to 20% of cases when value traders act beyond 1 tick, against 5% expected: rare blowouts of the spread to 20 ticks and more, when the book is briefly empty, carry the average, and blocks of time did not help. Bybit has such blowouts too (10 October). The share of quotes wider than one tick is bounded by construction. A first replacement, the rank of the minute among 501 fake marks 5 to 55 s off it, was itself wrong in the simulation: neighbouring fake marks share most of their window, so they count as about twenty draws, not 501, and gave p = 0.002 in a world with no clock; fake marks 5 to 7 s after the minute also still sat in a maker's widened window. Fake marks 10 to 50 s away, averaged and compared day by day, with the days as the units of the test, passed |t| > 2 in 3 to 8% of cases in a 24-hour world with no clock, each second of the minute taken in turn as the mark, about the 6% expected.
  *Changed on 2026-11-07, before any out-of-sample data was used.* H11 first read: in the 60 s after the mid crosses its 50-minute average, signed taker flow is more on the side of the crossing than at matched moments. In the teaching simulation (step 5), where the trend follower is known, it fails for two reasons. The value traders take the follower's flow back at once (in the 5 s after a flip it buys a net 0.61 BTC, they sell 0.40), so net flow barely moves; and against the mid itself the rule flips back within seconds (median 4 s), so the reversal falls inside the window. Its control is also biased: with no follower, the 5 s after a crossing show −0.13 BTC (t −4.2). Counting only the size on the crossing's side finds the follower only when the measure guesses its rule; with the wrong rule it finds nothing or the wrong sign. Real trend followers that work on bars act at the bar's close, so the clock is common to them where the rule is not, and the burst at clock marks is documented (Kim and Hansen, 2026). In the simulation, with crowds of followers on minute and 5-minute bars: the extra size at the mark is found from about 1% of taker size (t ≈ 10), but a maker widening at the mark raises it a little with no follower (t 2.1), so it is reported, not tested; flow with the trend needs a share of about 1.4% to 6% for t above 2.5; the spread after the mark widens with the followers alone (they walk the book), while the spread before it widens only if makers expect them, so H11b uses the spread before. These measures are run on the simulation with the truth known, over crowd sizes and value traders' triggers, before they touch real data.
- **H12 (a sliced order's footprint).** The share of taker size in runs of eight or more orders of one exact size on one side, with near-regular gaps (coefficient of variation below 0.2, mean gap 1 s or more), is larger than in the same day with the order of sizes shuffled within 5-minute blocks, out of sample.
  *Changed on 2026-11-07, before any out-of-sample data was used.* The rule first read five orders. In the teaching simulation (step 4), where the true TWAP is known, five orders with a coefficient of variation below 0.2 occur by chance among other orders as often as in real slicing: the share found was no larger than with sizes shuffled, and only about half of it was TWAP. With eight or more, 93 to 97% of what is found is TWAP, for coefficients from 0.1 to 0.2 and for slices exactly 30 s apart or randomised by up to 20%; with no TWAP, 0.01% of taker size is found. The rule finds about 40% of the TWAP's size, so the share it reports is a floor. H16, which recognises a run from its third slice, will need the same check in the simulation before it is run.

- **Style shares, used by H13 to H15.** Each taker order goes to one footprint, in this order: sliced (H12), sniper-like (H10), large (1 BTC or more), other. The clock-like share cannot be given order by order: it is the extra taker size in the first 3 s after the marks of H11 over seconds 10 to 49 of the same minutes, as a share of taker size, with the part on the trend's side reported. All shares are of taker size, and are shares of footprints, not of traders.
- **H13 (do styles come at expected times?).** For the sliced, sniper-like and clock-like shares: the profile over the 24 hours in sample has a positive rank correlation with the profile out of sample. Reported: the shares day by day over the 45 days, in sample against out of sample, so that a change of style shows.
- **H14 (in what circumstances?).** Between the most and the least volatile third of hours (thirds fixed in sample), within the same hour of the day: the sniper-like and clock-like shares are higher, the sliced share is lower, out of sample. Reason: a move on Binance is likelier in volatile hours, and trends that bar rules follow are larger, and someone working a large order may wait. Reported: the same by depth at the best and by spread.
- **H15 (does one style set off another?).** A Hawkes process with one type per footprint, extending Lecture 20's: the sum of the effects of one type on another (the off-diagonal branching ratios) is larger than with the labels shuffled within 5-minute blocks, out of sample. Each pair is reported after correction. What this shows is one style following another, not cause: both can answer the same move, and the footprints are partly defined by past prices, which is what the shuffling and the simulation with a known trigger are there to bound.

## Added 2026-11-05, before any of their results: patterns that can be recognised as they start

**What prompted this, stated before any out-of-sample result.** A quick look at three in-sample days (24 September, 1 and 7 October) found same-size taker orders about 30.22 s apart, with gaps that vary by a few milliseconds: for example, sells of exactly 20 BTC ten times at 30.215 s. Pieces join up to round parents, e.g. 1.667 BTC × 40 and 1.666 BTC × 20 = 100 BTC in 30 minutes, and 0.834 BTC × 40 and 0.833 BTC × 80 = 100 BTC in one hour. Bybit documents a TWAP strategy order with a 30-second default interval and a minimum run of 5 minutes, which is 10 slices. This is consistent with that tool, and we cannot prove it; it also offers randomised sizes (±20%), which an exact-size search misses. Out-of-sample days have not been looked at.

**A run.** Taker orders on one side, of equal size within 0.01 BTC, with a gap within 1 s of 30.22 s (other regular gaps reported apart). It is **recognised** at its third order, when two equal gaps have been seen, using only what was known then.

- **H16 (a run can be recognised as it starts).** Out of sample, recognised runs of 1 BTC a slice or more: a next slice arrives within 100 ms of the predicted time more often than at the same delay after matched non-runs of the same size and side. Reported: the share with a next slice, the number of slices that remain, the false alarms, and the share of taker size in such runs, day by day and by hour.
- **H17 (does the market see it coming?).** The net response to a slice recognised as part of a run, the fourth or later, is smaller than to a single order of the same size and same relative size x at a moment with no run under way. Reason: flow that can be expected should move the price less than a surprise. Reported: whether the mid, the depth at the best on the side to be hit and other takers' flow change in the 1 s before a predicted slice, against the same second in matched moments. Also the path over a whole run and after its end: this is the real test case for layers C and D, so H7 is reported on these runs beside its own.
- **H18 (levels).** Depth standing at prices that are multiples of 1,000 USDT is larger than at the neighbouring non-round prices (3 to 10 ticks away either side), out of sample, within 1% of the mid; the same for multiples of 100 USDT, reported. Reason: Lecture 18 saw walls at 111,000 USDT, and Osler (2003) found customer orders in currencies clustered at round numbers (to be checked against the source). Reported: pulls and additions at those prices as the mid comes near, and whether the price stops at them more than at neighbouring prices.

**Times, already measured.** Lecture 18: pulls in 7% of updates after a quiet second, 79% after a move of more than 5 bp, and 1.4 times the day's average in the first seconds of a minute. Lecture 19: at the start of minutes :00 and :30 pulls burst and depth jumps to 1.15 times the hour's average. Section 5 uses them as market-maker activity by time and adds them to H13 and H14 as a fourth profile.

The correction runs over one p-value each for H16, H17, H18 and H19 as well.

## Added 2026-11-05: a method for reading a market

The aim of this part of the lecture is to teach a method for reading a market, usable on any market with a public book and trades, not a catalogue of patterns. Section 5 is built around it.

| Step | The question | What to look at | From |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. The state | Is there anything to trade against, and which way does it lean? | Spread, depth, the imbalance at the best | Lectures 18, 19 |
| 2. The flow | Who is pushing, and how hard? | Order-flow imbalance, taker size, how much reacts to what | Lecture 20 |
| 3. The styles | What is at work now? | A run under way, sniper-like orders, trend-like flow, market makers pulling or refilling | H10 to H16 |
| 4. Clock and levels | What is about to come? | Time to the next :00 or :30, the next slice of a run, round prices nearby | Lecture 19, H13, H16, H18 |
| 5. The circumstances | Is this an ordinary hour? | Volatility and depth against their usual values, a cascading pattern | H14, Section 6 |
| 6. The read | What do we expect next, and what would prove us wrong? | One sentence, written before looking ahead, then checked | H19 |

Taught on three real moments, each read in full using only what was known then: a calm hour, a run under way, and the cascade of 10 October. Then the reader does it alone, as an exercise on another market.

- **H19 (is a reading worth anything?).** A read, the measures of steps 1 to 5 known at each moment, with a simple model fitted in sample, predicts the size of the mid's move and the 10-s markout of Lecture 21's resting order over the next minute with a lower mean squared error than volatility and the time of day alone, out of sample. The primary measure is the size of the move. If this fails, the method describes a market and forecasts nothing, and the lecture says so. Reported: which steps add anything, one at a time.

## Changed on 2026-11-09, after §3 and §4 were computed, before any result of H16 to H22: what our quotes meet

**Why.** H16 and H17 concern runs, which are about 1% of taker size out of sample (§3), and those of 1 BTC a slice or more, which H16 tests, are a small part of that; H16 also needs its own check in the simulation first. For the market maker of Lecture 24 the more useful question is what each footprint costs a resting order, and whether a quoter can step aside in time. H16 and H17 become an exercise and a note; H21 and H22 take their two places in the correction, which stays at twelve p-values. Out-of-sample data have been used for H10 to H14 (§3, §4), including the share of size and the net R(10 s) of sniper-like orders; nothing has been computed on the makers' result or on delays.

Footprints as in §4: each taker order is sliced (H12's runs), else sniper-like (H10), else large (1 BTC or more), else other. All taker orders, all sizes.

- **H21 (who the makers lose to).** Every taker order fills resting orders. The makers' **adverse move** on an order is its size times the mid's move in the order's direction 10 s later, gross (not net of controls: it is what the resting orders lose). Out of sample, the share of the total adverse move that comes from sniper-like orders is larger than their share of taker size. Statistic: share of the adverse move less share of size, bootstrap over days. Reported: the same for every footprint; the makers' result per BTC, the taker's cost from the mid less the adverse move, at 1, 10 and 60 s, by footprint.
- **H22 (can a quoter step aside in time?).** A **Binance move** is a Binance trade whose price is 2 bp or more from the last price at least 100 ms before it, the first in 1 s. Out of sample, the share of Binance moves followed within 50 ms (the central latency of Lecture 21) by a Bybit taker order in the move's direction is larger than the same share at random moments of the same hour of the same day, with a random direction. Statistic: the difference of the two shares, bootstrap over days. If it holds, a quoter at 50 ms cannot count on pulling a stale quote before it is taken. Reported: the distribution of the delay, the shares within 10, 50 and 100 ms, and the same for moves of 5 bp or more. The two exchanges' clocks are not synchronised to the millisecond; an offset of a few ms moves the shares at 10 ms more than at 50 or 100 ms.
